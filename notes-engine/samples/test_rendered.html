Topic 1
Wind Energy — Power, Forces & Calculations
1.1

Why Air Density Matters First

Before calculating any wind power, you need to know the density of the air at your site. This might seem like a small detail but it is not — wind power is directly proportional to air density. Denser air carries more mass per cubic metre, and more mass means more kinetic energy per second flowing through the turbine blades.

Air density is not a fixed constant you memorise. It depends on pressure and temperature at the site. Your lecturer always derives it fresh from the ideal gas law.

ρ
Air Density (rho) — kg/m³
Mass of air per unit volume. At sea level and 15°C, ρ ≈ 1.225 kg/m³. Cold air is denser (heavier molecules pack tighter). High-altitude air is less dense. For wind power calculations, always compute ρ from site pressure and temperature — do not assume 1.225 unless given standard conditions.
Air Density from Ideal Gas Law
\[ \rho = \frac{P}{RT} \]
P = atmospheric pressure (Pa) — 1 atm = 1.01325 × 10⁵ Pa
R = specific gas constant for air = 0.287 kJ/kg·K = 287 J/kg·K
T = absolute temperature (Kelvin) = °C + 273

For standard conditions (1 atm, 15°C): ρ = 101325 ÷ (287 × 288) = 1.226 kg/m³

1.2

The Five Wind Power Quantities

Your lecturer's wind turbine question always asks for five things in order. Understanding what each one is before calculating it is what separates someone who understands from someone who is guessing.

P/A
Total Power Density — W/m²
The total kinetic energy flux through 1 m² of the air stream per second. This is the maximum possible power available in the wind — before the turbine extracts any of it.
Total Wind Power Density
\[ \frac{P_{total}}{A} = \frac{1}{2}\rho V^3 \]
V = wind speed (m/s). Power varies with V³ — doubling wind speed gives 8× more power.
16/27
Betz Limit = 59.3%
The theoretical maximum fraction of wind power that any turbine can ever extract. Derived by Albert Betz in 1919.
Maximum Power Density (Betz Limit)
\[ \frac{P_{max}}{A} = \frac{8}{27}\rho V^3 \]
Note: 8/27 = 0.296, which is exactly (16/27)/2 — consistent with the Betz derivation.
Reasonably Obtainable Power Density
\[ \frac{P}{A} = \eta \cdot \frac{P_{total}}{A} = 0.35 \times \frac{1}{2}\rho V^3 \]
Real turbines achieve 35–45% efficiency.
Total Power Output
\[ P = \frac{P}{A} \times A = \frac{P}{A} \times \frac{\pi D^2}{4} \]
A = swept area of rotor (m²).
Torque at Maximum Efficiency
\[ T_{max} = \frac{2}{27} \cdot \frac{\rho D V^3}{N/60} \cdot \pi \]
N = rotational speed in rpm.
Maximum Axial Thrust
\[ F_{max} = \frac{\pi}{9} \cdot \rho D^2 V^2 \]
The factor π/9 comes from the Betz derivation for the thrust coefficient.
Worked Example 9.1
Wind Turbine — D = 120m, V = 15 m/s, 40 rpm
Given
  • Wind at 1 standard atmosphere and 15°C
  • Wind speed V = 15 m/s | Diameter D = 120m
  • Operating speed N = 40 rpm at max efficiency
  • Efficiency η = 35% | Gas constant R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K
Step 1
Calculate air density ρ
Using ideal gas law:
\[ \rho = \frac{P}{RT} = \frac{1.01325\times10^5}{287\times(15+273)} = \frac{101325}{82656} \approx \mathbf{1.226\ \text{kg/m}^3} \]
Step 2
(i) Total Power Density
Evaluated at V = 15 m/s:
\[ \frac{P_{total}}{A} = \frac{1}{2}\rho V^3 = \frac{1}{2}\times1.226\times15^3 = 0.613\times3375 \approx \mathbf{2{,}069\ \text{W/m}^2} \]
Step 3
(ii) Maximum Power Density (Betz Limit)
Betz limit calculation:
\[ \frac{P_{max}}{A} = \frac{8}{27}\rho V^3 = \frac{8}{27}\times1.226\times3375 = 0.296\times4137.75 \approx \mathbf{1{,}226\ \text{W/m}^2} \]
Step 4
(iii) Reasonably Obtainable Power Density (η = 35%)
At 35% efficiency:
\[ \frac{P}{A} = 0.35\times2069 \approx \mathbf{724\ \text{W/m}^2} \]
Step 5
(iv) Total Power P
Swept area A = πD²/4 = 11,310 m²:
\[ P = 724\times\frac{\pi\times120^2}{4} = 724\times11{,}310 \approx \mathbf{8{,}184\ \text{kW}} \]
Step 6
(v) Torque at Maximum Efficiency
Angular velocity ω = 2πN/60 = 4.189 rad/s:
\[ T_{max} = \frac{2}{27}\times\frac{\rho\times\pi\times D\times V^3}{N/60} = \frac{2\times1.226\times\pi\times120\times3375}{27\times(40/60)} \approx \mathbf{55{,}170\ \text{N·m}} \]
Step 7
(v) Maximum Axial Thrust
Thrust calculation:
\[ F_{max} = \frac{\pi}{9}\rho D^2 V^2 = \frac{\pi}{9}\times1.226\times120^2\times15^2 \approx \mathbf{1{,}385{,}870\ \text{N}} \]
✓ Summary: ρ = 1.226 kg/m³ | P/A = 2,069 W/m² | Betz max = 1,226 W/m² | At 35%: 724 W/m² | Total P = 8,184 kW | Torque = 55,170 N·m | Axial thrust = 1,385,870 N

Why is axial thrust important to engineers designing a wind turbine tower?
Axial thrust is the horizontal pushing force the wind exerts on the rotor in the direction of wind flow. This force must be transmitted down through the tower into the foundation. For a 120m turbine at 15 m/s, this force is approximately 1.4 million Newtons — enough to topple the tower if the structure is not strong enough. Engineers use the axial thrust calculation to design tower wall thickness, guy-wires, and foundation size to safely resist this overturning force.
Topic Active Recall
Mechanism
State the full calculation sequence for a wind turbine problem. What is calculated first and why?
1. ρ = P/RT — air density first. Everything else depends on it. Do not assume 1.225 — always derive from given P and T.

2. P/A = ½ρV³ — total power density. The maximum possible energy per m² of wind stream.

3. P/A = (8/27)ρV³ — Betz maximum. The physical ceiling no turbine can exceed.

4. P/A = η × ½ρV³ — realistic power density using given efficiency (35%).

5. P = (P/A) × πD²/4 — total power by multiplying density by swept area.

6. Torque and axial thrust formulas — mechanical forces on the structure.

Topic 2
Family Biogas Digester — Design & Power Output
2.1

What a Biogas Digester Actually Is

A biogas digester is an airtight sealed tank where organic waste — usually animal dung or agricultural residue — is mixed with water and left to ferment in the absence of oxygen. Bacteria inside break down the organic material and produce biogas as a by-product. The biogas (mostly methane) is piped off and burned for cooking or electricity.

Analogy

The Mechanical Stomach Analogy: You feed organic waste in one end (daily input). The bacteria process it over several days (retention time). Gas comes out the top. Spent slurry comes out the bottom — this slurry is rich in nutrients and makes excellent fertiliser. Nothing is wasted.

⚡ Quick Check — What a Biogas Digester Actually Is 1 question(s)
Explain how a biogas digester operates like a mechanical stomach.
Organic waste mixed with water enters daily (input), bacteria ferment it anaerobically over a fixed retention time (digestion), biogas is captured from the top for energy, and nutrient-rich slurry exits at the bottom as fertiliser.
2.2

The Four Quantities — Input to Output

The family biogas calculation always follows the same four-step chain. Each step uses the result from the previous one.

mo
Daily Dry Matter Input (kg/day)
The mass of dry organic matter fed into the digester each day. For cattle dung: each cow produces about 2 kg of dry matter per day.
Vf
Daily Slurry Volume (m³/day)
The volume of slurry entering the digester per day. Slurry density ρin = 50 kg/m³ is a standard design constant for cattle dung slurry.
Daily Slurry Volume
\[ V_f = \frac{m_o}{\rho_{in}} \]
ρin = 50 kg/m³ (standard design constant for cattle dung slurry).
tr
Retention Time (days)
How long the organic material must remain inside the digester for complete fermentation. Typical value is 20 days.
Digester Volume
\[ V_d = V_f \times t_r \]
Vd = Vf × tr. The tank must hold tr days' worth of slurry simultaneously.
Daily Biogas Production
\[ V_b = C \times m_o \]
C = biogas yield (m³ per kg of dry matter). For cattle dung: C = 0.24 m³/kg.
Daily Power Available from Digester
\[ E = \eta \cdot H_m \cdot F_m \cdot V_b \]
η = burner efficiency (0.6), Hm = 28 MJ/m³, Fm = 0.8 (80% methane fraction), Vb = daily biogas volume.
Worked Example 9.2
Family Biogas Digester — 5 Cows, 20 Days Retention
Given
  • 5 cows | dry matter per cow = 2 kg/day | retention time tr = 20 days
  • Slurry density ρin = 50 kg/m³ | Biogas yield C = 0.24 m³/kg
  • Methane fraction Fm = 0.8 (80%) | Burner efficiency η = 0.6 (60%)
  • Heat of combustion of methane Hm = 28 MJ/m³
Step 1
Daily dry matter input mo
5 cows × 2 kg/day:
\[ m_o = 2\ \text{kg/day}\times5 = \mathbf{10\ \text{kg/day}} \]
Step 2
Daily slurry volume Vf
Dividing by slurry density 50 kg/m³:
\[ V_f = \frac{m_o}{\rho_{in}} = \frac{10}{50} = \mathbf{0.2\ \text{m}^3\text{/day}} \]
Step 3
Digester volume Vd
Retention time 20 days:
\[ V_d = V_f\times t_r = 0.2\times20 = \mathbf{4\ \text{m}^3} \]
Step 4
Daily biogas production Vb
Yield C = 0.24 m³/kg:
\[ V_b = C\times m_o = 0.24\times10 = \mathbf{2.4\ \text{m}^3\text{/day}} \]
Step 5
Daily energy output E and continuous thermal power
Energy and power calculation:
\[ E = \eta\cdot H_m\cdot F_m\cdot V_b = 0.6\times28\times0.8\times2.4 = \mathbf{32.25\ \text{MJ/day}} \]
✓ A 4 m³ digester fed by 5 cows produces 2.4 m³ of biogas per day, delivering 32.25 MJ/day — equivalent to a 373W heater running 24 hours a day.

Why is the methane fraction Fm needed in the power formula?
Biogas is a mixture (60–80% CH₄, 20–40% CO₂). Only the methane fraction burns and releases heat — CO₂ is inert. Fm selects only the combustible methane fraction so power is not overestimated.

Application
State the 5-step chain for a family biogas digester problem.
1. mo = animals × kg/day per animal → daily dry matter input

2. Vf = mo / ρin → daily slurry volume

3. Vd = Vf × tr → digester volume

4. Vb = C × mo → daily biogas production

5. E = η × Hm × Fm × Vb → daily useful energy output.

Topic 3
Village Community Biogas Plant — Supply, Demand & Digester Sizing
3.1

The Big Picture — Supply vs Demand Logic

The village biogas problem is bigger than the family digester, but the logic is the same — just scaled up and with an important extra step: you must verify that your supply of gas meets your community's demand before sizing the digesters.

Key Insight

Why Design for Winter? Gas production from biogas digesters slows in cold weather because bacterial activity decreases at lower temperatures. Winter yield = 42 litres/kg vs summer yield = 55 litres/kg. You must design the plant to meet demand during winter — the worst case.

3.2

Digester Sizing — Volume and Dimensions

Once the number of digesters is chosen (e.g. 4 digesters), each digester is sized by calculating daily charge volume, applying retention time, adding a 10% safety margin, and solving for H = D geometry.

1090
Slurry Density for Village Digesters — kg/m³
In community plants, dung is mixed 1:1 with water by mass. The resulting slurry density is 1090 kg/m³.
Volume of Daily Charge per Digester
\[ V_{charge} = \frac{m_{dung} + m_{water}}{1090} \]
Dung:water = 1:1 mix by mass.
Digester Volume (with 10% safety factor)
\[ V_d = 1.1 \times V_{charge} \times t_r \]
1.1 factor adds 10% safety margin.
Digester Dimensions (H = D for large plants)
\[ V_d = \frac{\pi}{4}D^3 \quad\Rightarrow\quad D = \sqrt[3]{\frac{V_d}{0.785}} \]
H = D geometry standard rule.
Worked Example 9.3
Village Biogas Plant — 98 Families, Mixed Livestock
Given
  • 98 families (490 total persons) | 102 cows, 124 oxen, 52 buffalo, 3 pigs
  • Cooking: 0.227 m³/person/day | Lighting: 0.126 m³/lamp·hr
  • Winter yield: 42 L/kg | Retention time tr = 30 days | 4 digesters | H = D
Step 1
Total dung available
Cows (102×10) + Oxen (124×12) + Buffalo (52×15) + Pigs (3×2) =
\[ 1020 + 1488 + 780 + 6 = \mathbf{3300\ \text{kg/day}} \]
Step 2
Gas supply (winter)
42 L/kg × 3300 kg = 138,600 L/day =
\[ \mathbf{138.6\ \text{m}^3\text{/day}} \]
Step 3
Total demand and supply check
Cooking (111.23 m³) + Lighting (24.69 m³) = 136.92 m³/day.
\[ 138.6\ \text{m}^3\text{/day} > 136.92\ \text{m}^3\text{/day} \quad\checkmark \]
Step 4
Digester dimensions (4 digesters, H = D)
Vd = 1.1 × 1.51 × 30 = 49.53 m³. D³ = 49.53 / 0.785 = 63.1 m³:
\[ D = \sqrt[3]{63.1} \approx \mathbf{4\ \text{m}}, \quad H = \mathbf{4\ \text{m}} \]
✓ Each of the 4 digesters has diameter D = 4m and height H = 4m. Winter gas supply (138.6 m³/day) satisfies demand (136.92 m³/day).
⚡ Quick Check — Digester Sizing — Volume and Dimensions 1 question(s)
In the digester volume formula, why is a 10% safety factor (1.1 multiplier) applied?
The 10% safety factor accounts for minor gas/slurry leakage, minor population/livestock growth, measurement uncertainties in dung supply, and demand fluctuations.

Comparison
State the full sequence for a village biogas plant problem from animal census to digester dimensions.
1. Total dung: sum dung across all animal species.
2. Gas supply: dung × winter yield (litres/kg) converted to m³/day.
3. Gas demand: cooking + lighting.
4. Verify supply > demand.
5. Dung per digester: total dung ÷ number of digesters.
6. Daily charge volume: (dung + equal water) ÷ 1090 kg/m³.
7. Digester volume: 1.1 × daily charge × 30 days.
8. Dimensions: D = (V / 0.785)^(1/3), H = D.
