Topic 1
Geothermal Hydrothermal Steam Plant & Tidal Energy
1.1

The Geothermal Steam Plant — How It Works

A vapour-dominated hydrothermal plant uses steam from underground reservoirs to drive a turbine and generate electricity. The key difference from a conventional steam power plant is the heat source: instead of burning fuel to boil water, geothermal heat from the Earth's interior produces steam naturally.

The calculation involves four quantities in sequence:

  1. Enthalpy values from steam tables at each state point
  2. Actual turbine work = isentropic work × polytropic efficiency
  3. Steam flow rate from plant output and actual turbine work
  4. Cooling water flow from condenser energy balance
  5. Plant efficiency and heat rate
H
Enthalpy — kcal/kg
The total energy content of the steam at a given state (pressure, temperature, and quality). Read directly from steam tables given pressure and quality (x). Throttling is a special process where enthalpy is conserved even as pressure drops — so H1 = H2 = H3 across a throttle valve.
x
Dryness Fraction (Quality)
The fraction of the steam that is vapour (dry) vs liquid water. x = 0 = all liquid. x = 1.0 = all dry steam. x = 0.838 means 83.8% vapour, 16.2% liquid. Used to find enthalpy at wet conditions: H = hf + x·hfg, where hf = enthalpy of saturated liquid and hfg = latent heat of vaporisation.
Throttling — The Key Process

In this plant, steam at 31 kg/cm² is throttled to 9.55 kg/cm² before entering the turbine. Throttling is a constant enthalpy (isenthalpic) process — pressure drops but enthalpy stays the same. So H at 31 kg/cm² = H entering turbine at 9.55 kg/cm². This simplifies the starting point: read H from tables at 31 kg/cm², and that same value applies at turbine inlet.

Key Formulas
\[ \text{Actual turbine work} = \eta_{poly} \times (H_3 - H_{4s}) \]
\[ \dot{m}_s = \frac{\text{Plant output (kcal/hr)}}{\text{Actual turbine work} \times \eta_{mech-elec}} \]
\[ \dot{m}_7(h_5 - h_7) = \dot{m}_s(h_4 - h_6) \quad\text{(condenser energy balance)} \]
\[ \eta_{plant} = \frac{\text{Actual turbine work} \times \eta_{mech-elec}}{H_3 - H_6} \]
\[ \text{Heat rate} = \frac{860}{\eta_{plant}}\ \text{kcal/kWh} \]
860 kcal = 1 kWh (standard conversion). Heat rate = heat input per unit of electrical output. Lower heat rate = more efficient plant.
Worked Example 11.1
250MW Geothermal Hydrothermal Steam Plant
Given
  • 250MW plant | Saturated steam at 31 kg/cm² shut-off
  • Steam throttled to turbine at 9.55 kg/cm²
  • Direct contact condenser at 0.35 kg/cm² | Cooling tower exit at 25°C
  • Turbine polytropic efficiency = 0.80
  • Combined mechanical & electrical efficiency = 0.90
  • Reinjection occurs before cooling tower
  • From steam tables: H1 = 669.7 kcal/kg at 31 kg/cm²
  • At condenser 0.35 kg/cm²: x = 0.838, hf = 72 kcal/kg, hfg = 556 kcal/kg
  • h6 = 25 kcal/kg (cooling tower exit, saturated liquid at 25°C)
Step 1
Enthalpy at turbine inlet H3 (throttling: H1 = H2 = H3)
\[ H_3 = H_1 = \mathbf{669.7\ \text{kcal/kg}} \]
Throttling conserves enthalpy. No calculation needed — read from steam tables at 31 kg/cm².
Step 2
Isentropic enthalpy at condenser H4s (wet steam, x = 0.838)
\[ H_{4s} = h_f + x \cdot h_{fg} = 72 + 0.838 \times 556 = 72 + 465.7 = \mathbf{537.7 \approx 538\ \text{kcal/kg}} \]
Step 3
Isentropic turbine work and actual turbine work
\[ W_{isen} = H_3 - H_{4s} = 669.7 - 538 = 131.7\ \text{kcal/kg} \]
\[ W_{actual} = \eta_{poly} \times W_{isen} = 0.80 \times 131.7 = \mathbf{105.36\ \text{kcal/kg}} \]
Step 4
Actual enthalpy at turbine exit H4
\[ H_4 = H_3 - W_{actual} = 669.7 - 105.36 = \mathbf{564.34\ \text{kcal/kg}} \]
Step 5
Steam (turbine) mass flow rate ṁs
Convert 250 MW to kcal/hr: 250 × 10³ kW × 860 kcal/kWh = 215 × 10⁶ kcal/hr
\[ \dot{m}_s = \frac{250 \times 0.860 \times 10^6}{105.36 \times 0.90} = \frac{215 \times 10^6}{94.82} = \mathbf{1.838 \times 10^6\ \text{kg/hr}} \]
Step 6
Turbine volume flow rate
Specific volume at turbine inlet (9.55 kg/cm²) = 0.22 m³/kg
\[ \text{Volume flow} = \frac{\dot{m}_s \times V_s}{60} = \frac{1.838 \times 10^6 \times 0.22}{60} = \mathbf{6.732 \times 10^3\ \text{m}^3\text{/min}} \]
Step 7
Cooling water flow rate ṁ7 (condenser energy balance)
H5 = hf at condenser pressure = 72 kcal/kg (saturated liquid). h6 = 25 kcal/kg. h7 = h6 = 25 kcal/kg (cooling water inlet).
\[ \dot{m}_7(h_5 - h_7) = \dot{m}_s(H_4 - H_6) \]
\[ \dot{m}_7 = \frac{\dot{m}_s(H_4 - H_6)}{h_5 - h_7} = \frac{1.838 \times 10^6 \times (564.34 - 72)}{72 - 25} = \frac{1.838 \times 10^6 \times 492.34}{47} \]
\[ \dot{m}_7 = \mathbf{19.21 \times 10^6\ \text{kg/hr}} \]
Step 8
Plant efficiency and heat rate
\[ \text{Heat added} = H_3 - H_6 = 669.7 - 72 = 597.7\ \text{kcal/kg} \]
\[ \eta_{plant} = \frac{W_{actual} \times \eta_{mech-elec}}{\text{Heat added}} = \frac{105.36 \times 0.90}{597.7} = \frac{94.82}{597.7} = \mathbf{15.86\%} \]
\[ \text{Heat rate} = \frac{860}{\eta_{plant}} = \frac{860}{0.1586} = \mathbf{5422\ \text{kcal/kWh}} \]
✓ Steam flow = 1.838×10⁶ kg/hr  |  Volume flow = 6,732 m³/min  |  Cooling water = 19.21×10⁶ kg/hr  |  Plant efficiency = 15.86%  |  Heat rate = 5,422 kcal/kWh
1.2

Tidal Power — Simple Basin Method

A tidal barrage traps seawater at high tide, then releases it through turbines as the sea level drops. The energy comes from the potential energy difference between the trapped water and the sea level.

R
Tidal Range — metres
The total height difference between high tide and low tide. The energy available is proportional to R² — doubling the tidal range gives 4× more energy. Sites with large tidal ranges (Bay of Fundy 16m, Severn Estuary 7–13m) are most attractive.
Tidal Energy — Simple Basin
\[ W = \int_r^R \rho g A h\, dh = \frac{1}{2}\rho g A(R^2 - r^2) \]
A = basin area (m²)  |  R = tidal range (m)  |  r = minimum head for turbine operation (m)
ρ = seawater density ≈ 1025 kg/m³  |  g = 9.81 m/s²

The turbine stops when the head falls below r = 3m (given). So usable energy is from head R down to r, not all the way to zero.
Worked Example 11.2
Simple Basin Tidal Power Plant
Given
  • Basin area A = 30 × 10⁶ m²
  • Tidal range R = 12m
  • Turbines stop below head r = 3m
  • Turbine-generator efficiency η = 0.73
  • ρseawater = 1025 kg/m³ (use 1000 kg/m³ as lecturer’s approximation)
Step 1
Theoretical work W (integration from r to R)
\[ W = \frac{1}{2}\rho g A(R^2 - r^2) = \frac{1}{2} \times 1000 \times 9.81 \times 30 \times 10^6 \times (12^2 - 3^2) \]
\[ = \frac{1}{2} \times 1000 \times 9.81 \times 30 \times 10^6 \times (144 - 9) \]
\[ = \frac{1}{2} \times 9810 \times 30 \times 10^6 \times 135 = \frac{1}{2} \times 3.972 \times 10^{13} \]
\[ W = \mathbf{1.986 \times 10^{13}\ \text{J per tidal cycle}} \]
Step 2
Electrical energy output (apply efficiency)
\[ E = \eta \times W = 0.73 \times 1.986 \times 10^{13} = \mathbf{1.450 \times 10^{13}\ \text{J}} \]
\[ = \frac{1.450 \times 10^{13}}{3.6 \times 10^6} = \mathbf{4.03 \times 10^6\ \text{kWh per tidal cycle}} \]
✓ Each tidal cycle generates approximately 4.03 × 10⁶ kWh of electrical energy. With roughly 2 cycles per day, the plant produces about 8 × 10⁶ kWh per day — equivalent to a significant contribution to a regional grid.

Sequence
State the 8-step sequence for solving a geothermal steam plant problem. What is calculated at each step and which formula is used?
1. H3 from throttling: H3 = H1 (read from steam tables, throttling conserves enthalpy)
2. H4s from steam tables: H4s = hf + x·hfg at condenser pressure
3. Isentropic work: Wisen = H3 − H4s
4. Actual turbine work: Wactual = ηpoly × Wisen
5. Actual exit enthalpy: H4 = H3 − Wactual
6. Steam flow rate: ṁs = plant output (kcal/hr) / (Wactual × ηmech-elec)
7. Cooling water flow: condenser energy balance: ṁ7(h5−h7) = ṁs(H4−H6)
8. Efficiency & heat rate: η = Wactual×ηmech / (H3−H6); Heat rate = 860/η
Topic 2
Power Station Economics — Tariffs, Costs & Input-Output Curves
2.1

Cost Structure of a Power Station

Running a power station involves two fundamentally different types of costs, and understanding this distinction is the foundation of all power economics calculations.

FC
Fixed Costs
Costs that are paid regardless of how much electricity is generated. They depend on the physical size and value of the plant, not its output. Examples: loan interest on capital cost, depreciation (plant wear over time), insurance, taxes, land lease, maintenance contract (fixed portion). These are paid even when the plant is idle.
RC
Running Costs
Costs that scale with electricity generated. The more you generate, the more you spend. Examples: fuel cost (coal, gas, diesel), operating labour costs (variable portion), plant maintenance (variable). These are zero when the plant is off.
Key Cost Formulas
\[ \text{Cost per kW per year} = \frac{\text{Annual fixed cost}}{\text{Maximum demand (kW)}} \]
\[ \text{Cost per kWh} = \frac{\text{Annual running cost}}{\text{Annual energy generated (kWh)}} \]
\[ \text{Total cost per kWh} = \frac{\text{Annual fixed cost} + \text{Annual running cost}}{\text{Annual energy generated}} \]
\[ \text{Annual energy} = \text{Average load} \times 8760\ \text{hrs} = \text{Max demand} \times \text{Load factor} \times 8760 \]
Load factor = average load / maximum demand. A high load factor means the plant runs near full capacity most of the time — spreading fixed costs over more kWh → lower cost per kWh.
2.2

Objectives and Requirements of Tariff

A tariff is the schedule of charges paid by electricity consumers. The objectives of a good tariff:

  • A net return on the total capital investment must be ensured
  • Recovery of cost of material, transportation, billing and collection costs
  • Recovery of cost of operation, supplies and maintenance
  • Recovery of capital investment in generation, transmission and distribution

The requirements of a good tariff:

  • Should be easy to understand
  • Should provide low rates for high consumption
  • Should be uniform over large population
  • Should encourage consumers with high load factors
  • Should take into account maximum demand and energy charges
  • Should provide incentive for using power during off-peak hours
  • Should apportion costs equitably to different categories of consumers

Types of tariff: Flat demand rate · Straight meter rate · Block meter rate · Wright demand rate · Hopkinson demand rate (two-part) · Doherty rate (three-part)

Worked Example 11.3
10MW Power Station — Cost per kW per Year and Cost per kWh
Given
  • Plant capacity = 10,000 kW  |  Capital cost = ₦1,200 per kW
  • Interest, insurance & taxes = 5% per annum  |  Depreciation = 5%
  • Primary distribution cost = ₦500,000  |  Interest on distribution = 5%
  • Fixed plant maintenance = ₦20,000/yr  |  Variable maintenance = ₦30,000/yr
  • Annual operating cost = ₦500,000  |  Annual coal cost = ₦4.4 per KN × 255,000 KN
  • Max demand = 9,000 kW  |  Annual load factor = 0.6
Step 1
Total capital cost and annual fixed charges
\[ \text{Total cost of plant} = 10{,}000 \times 1{,}200 = \text{₦}12 \times 10^6 \]
\[ \text{Annual interest, insurance, taxes} = 0.05 \times 12 \times 10^6 = \text{₦}600{,}000 \]
\[ \text{Annual depreciation} = 0.05 \times 12 \times 10^6 = \text{₦}600{,}000 \]
\[ \text{Annual charges on distribution} = 0.05 \times 500{,}000 = \text{₦}25{,}000 \]
\[ \text{Total fixed cost} = 600{,}000 + 600{,}000 + 25{,}000 + 20{,}000 = \mathbf{\text{₦}1{,}245{,}000} \]
Step 2
Annual running cost
\[ \text{Coal cost} = 4.4 \times 255{,}000 = \text{₦}1{,}122{,}000 \]
\[ \text{Total running cost} = 500{,}000 + 30{,}000 + 1{,}122{,}000 = \mathbf{\text{₦}1{,}652{,}000} \]
Step 3
Annual energy generated
\[ \text{Average load} = 9{,}000 \times 0.6 = 5{,}400\ \text{kW} \]
\[ \text{Annual energy} = 5{,}400 \times 365 \times 24 = \mathbf{47.3 \times 10^6\ \text{kWh}} \]
Step 4
(a) Cost of power generation per kW per year
\[ \frac{\text{Annual fixed cost}}{\text{Max demand}} = \frac{1{,}245{,}000}{9{,}000} = \mathbf{\text{₦}138.33\ \text{per kW per year}} \]
Step 5
(b) Cost per kWh generated
\[ \frac{\text{Annual running cost}}{\text{Annual energy}} = \frac{1{,}652{,}000}{47.3 \times 10^6} = \mathbf{\text{₦}0.035\ \text{per kWh}} \]
Step 6
(c) Total cost per kWh
\[ \frac{1{,}245{,}000 + 1{,}652{,}000}{47.3 \times 10^6} = \frac{2{,}897{,}000}{47.3 \times 10^6} = \mathbf{\text{₦}0.06\ \text{per kWh}} \]
✓ Cost per kW per year = ₦138.33  |  Running cost per kWh = ₦0.035  |  Total cost per kWh = ₦0.06
2.3

Input-Output Curve — Maximum Efficiency & Incremental Rate

The input-output curve of a power station relates the fuel input (I, in kJ/hr) to the power output (L, in MW). It is given as a polynomial equation.

Two key calculations follow from this curve:

  1. Load at maximum efficiency: Efficiency = Output/Input = L/I. To maximise efficiency, differentiate and set dL/dI = 0 (or equivalently d(I/L)/dL = 0).
  2. Incremental rate: IR = dI/dL — the extra fuel needed per extra MW of output. This is the slope of the input-output curve and can be used as a straight line for small load changes.
Input-Output Curve & Efficiency Condition
\[ I = 4 \times 10^6[10 + 8L + 0.4L^2] \]
\[ \eta = \frac{L}{I} = \frac{1}{4 \times 10^6\left[\frac{10}{L} + 8 + 0.4L\right]} \]
Maximum efficiency when (10/L + 8 + 0.4L) is minimum → differentiate and set to zero → −10/L² + 0.4 = 0 → L² = 25 → L = 5 MW
Worked Example 11.4
Input-Output Curve — Max Efficiency & Incremental Rate
Given
  • I = 4×10⁶[10 + 8L + 0.4L²] kJ/hr, L in MW
  • (i) Find load at maximum efficiency
  • (ii) Increase in input to raise output from 3 to 5 MW — by input-output curve and incremental rate curve
Part (i)
Load at maximum efficiency
Efficiency η = L/I = 1 / {4×10⁶[10/L + 8 + 0.4L]}. Maximum efficiency when denominator is minimum:
\[ \frac{d}{dL}\left(\frac{10}{L} + 8 + 0.4L\right) = 0 \Rightarrow -\frac{10}{L^2} + 0.4 = 0 \]
\[ L^2 = \frac{10}{0.4} = 25 \Rightarrow \mathbf{L = 5\ \text{MW}} \]
Maximum efficiency occurs at a load of 5 MW.
Part (ii-a)
Increase in input from input-output curve
\[ I_3 = 4 \times 10^6[10 + 8(3) + 0.4(9)] = 4 \times 10^6[10 + 24 + 3.6] = 4 \times 10^6 \times 37.6 = 150.4 \times 10^6\ \text{kJ/hr} \]
\[ I_5 = 4 \times 10^6[10 + 8(5) + 0.4(25)] = 4 \times 10^6[10 + 40 + 10] = 4 \times 10^6 \times 60 = 240 \times 10^6\ \text{kJ/hr} \]
\[ \Delta I = 240 \times 10^6 - 150.4 \times 10^6 = \mathbf{89.6 \times 10^6\ \text{kJ/hr}} \]
Part (ii-b)
Increase in input from incremental rate curve
Incremental rate IR = dI/dL = 4×10⁶[8 + 0.8L]. For small load change (3 to 5 MW), use average load L = (3+5)/2 = 4 MW:
\[ IR = 4 \times 10^6[8 + 0.8 \times 4] = 4 \times 10^6 \times 11.2 = 44.8 \times 10^6\ \text{kJ/hr per MW} \]
\[ \Delta I = IR \times \Delta L = 44.8 \times 10^6 \times (5-3) = \mathbf{89.6 \times 10^6\ \text{kJ/hr}} \]
✓ Both methods give the same answer: 89.6×10⁶ kJ/hr increase in input. This confirms that the incremental rate curve can be treated as a straight line for small output changes.

Topic 3
Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) Systems
3.1

What MHD Is and Why It Matters

Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) is the study of the flow of electrically conducting fluids in the presence of magnetic and electric fields. An MHD generator converts the kinetic energy of a high-speed conducting fluid directly into electrical energy — with no moving mechanical parts.

The fluid can be:

  • Hot ionised combustion gas (at 2300–2750°C) — open cycle systems
  • Liquid metals like sodium or potassium — closed cycle systems

MHD is considered one of the most promising direct energy conversion technologies for large-scale power generation because of its very high theoretical efficiency.

The Core Principle

Think of Faraday's law: when a conductor moves through a magnetic field, a voltage is induced. In a conventional generator, the conductor is a rotating wire coil. In an MHD generator, the conductor is the ionised gas or liquid metal itself — flowing at high speed through a strong magnetic field. No turbine, no rotating shaft, no mechanical friction. The moving charges directly generate electricity.

3.2

Open Cycle MHD System

In an open cycle MHD system:

  1. Fuel (oil, coal, natural gas) is burned in a combustion chamber. Air for combustion is preheated in an air preheater.
  2. The hot gases are seeded with a small amount of ionised alkali metal (potassium) to increase electrical conductivity. Ionisation occurs at temperatures of 2300–2750°C.
  3. The hot pressurised conducting gas passes through a convergent-divergent nozzle, accelerating to high velocity.
  4. The high-velocity conducting gas enters the MHD generator surrounded by powerful magnets. Expansion of the gas through the magnetic field induces a direct current (DC).
  5. The DC is converted to AC using an inverter.
  6. Exhaust gases pass through a heat exchanger to recover heat before discharge.
Open cycle MHD system. Seeded ionised gas at high velocity flows through a magnetic field, generating DC directly. No turbine or rotating parts.
3.3

Closed Cycle MHD System

In a closed cycle system, a liquid metal (typically potassium) is used as the working fluid:

  1. Liquid potassium is heated in a breeder reactor.
  2. It passes through a nozzle where velocity increases.
  3. Vapour is separated in a separator and condensed, then pumped back to the reactor.
  4. The high-velocity liquid metal passes through the MHD generator to produce DC power.
  5. Potassium leaving the MHD generator passes through a heat exchanger (boiler) to use remaining heat to run a conventional steam turbine, then returns to the reactor.

The closed cycle is more complex but uses no combustion gases and has lower environmental impact.

3.4

Merits and Demerits of MHD

Merits:

  • No moving parts — more reliable, no mechanical wear
  • No pollution — power produced is free of pollution
  • High efficiency — can reach about 50% compared to less than 40% for most efficient steam plants
  • Fast startup — as soon as it is started it can reach full power level
  • Lower operational cost
  • Smaller plant size — considerably smaller than conventional fossil fuel plants
  • Comparable capital cost to conventional steam plants
  • Better fuel utilisation

Demerits:

  • Very large and expensive magnets required — a major expense
  • High friction losses — friction loss may be as high as 12% of input
  • Heat transfer losses are significant
  • Electron reverse flow (short circuits) — electrons flow through the conducting fluid around the ends of the magnetic field. This can be reduced by: (i) increasing aspect ratio of the generator, (ii) permitting magnetic field poles to extend beyond electrodes, (iii) using insulated vanes in the fluid duct at inlet and outlet of the generator
⚡Quick Check — MHD2 questions
What is the key physical principle behind MHD power generation?
A Thermal expansion of gas drives a piston generator
B Faraday's law — a conducting fluid moving through a magnetic field generates an EMF directly
C Combustion gases rotate a turbine connected to a generator
D Electromagnetic radiation from hot gas is converted to electricity
Why must the combustion gases in an open cycle MHD system be seeded with an ionised alkali metal?
For Faraday's law to generate a useful voltage, the fluid flowing through the magnetic field must be electrically conducting. Normal combustion gases (CO₂, H₂O vapour, N₂) are poor electrical conductors, even at high temperatures. Adding a small amount of an ionised alkali metal (typically potassium) dramatically increases the electrical conductivity of the gas — potassium ionises easily at flame temperatures (2300–2750°C), releasing free electrons that carry current through the MHD channel. Without seeding, the conductivity is insufficient to generate practical power levels.

Comparison
Compare open cycle and closed cycle MHD systems. What is the working fluid in each, and what happens to the fluid after passing through the MHD generator?
Open Cycle:
Working fluid: Ionised combustion gas seeded with potassium.
After MHD generator: Exhaust gases pass through heat recovery (air preheater) then discharged to atmosphere. The system is open — fresh air and fuel enter continuously, exhaust leaves.

Closed Cycle:
Working fluid: Liquid metal (potassium).
After MHD generator: Potassium passes through a heat exchanger/boiler to drive a conventional steam turbine, then is pumped back to the breeder reactor for reheating. The system is closed — the same potassium circulates continuously. No exhaust to atmosphere.
